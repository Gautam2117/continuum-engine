# Why Dropbox paid for referrals in storage

Foundbeam narration draft. Alex and Sam are fictional. Use original desktop illustrations and no Dropbox interface screenshots. Historical figures and attribution come from the [editorial source ledger](dropbox-referrals-editorial.md).

## Opening

Alex has two ways to get Sam into Dropbox. One is to share the folder Sam needs. The other is to send a referral. Both can bring someone to Dropbox. Only one offers a reward for joining. That difference explains why the company had more than one way to grow, even when both actions started with the same person at the same desk.

*Visual: A folder and an invitation split into two paths. Keep both on screen as the story begins.*

## The search box

Imagine you’re sending a file back and forth with somebody. You fix a typo. They open yesterday’s version. Then someone sends a third copy, and the file name starts to look like a record of the argument. You know exactly what’s wrong. It’s less obvious what to type into a search box to make it stop.

In his 2010 talk, cofounder Drew Houston described this as a problem for Dropbox’s early acquisition efforts. Paid search could reach people who already knew what they wanted to look for. He recalled that display ads and landing pages also struggled. The team needed a way for somebody to see the product working before they had a name for the problem it solved.

*Visual: Three versions of the same file diverge, then resolve to one version on both desks.*

## The invitation

Now return to Alex and Sam. Suppose Alex has been keeping files in Dropbox and finds the service useful. Dropbox can ask Alex to tell a friend. But it can also give both Alex and that friend a reason to follow through. In the program Houston described, that reason was extra storage.

Picture what each person sees. Alex already knows what another bit of space is worth. Sam gets space to start using the product, too. The same action has a different benefit depending on which side of the invitation you’re on. And neither person has to learn what an unrelated reward is worth.

Make that distinction concrete. If Alex just shares a folder, Sam’s reason to click is the file inside. If Alex sends a referral, Sam is being invited to sign up, with a storage benefit attached. Someone can take either path. They can even arrive at the same service. Watching only the final signup would hide the difference between those two starting points.

*Visual: Show two storage meters increase without quantities. Then animate the shared file opening on a separate path.*

## Three numbers, three questions

Houston reported that Dropbox had 100,000 registered users in September 2008. By January 2010, he reported four million. Those numbers describe the size of the registered user base at two points in time. Think of them as two snapshots of a growing audience.

Then there’s a much larger number than you might expect to see next to an invite button. Houston’s April 2010 talk reported that users sent 2.8 million direct referral invitations in a 30-day period. A single person could send more than one invitation. Some recipients could ignore it. That number tells you how often users shared an invitation, with every send counted as an invitation.

The third measure asks a different question. On his results slide, Houston attributed 35 percent of daily signups to the referral program. He attributed another 20 percent to shared folders and other features that helped spread the product. Now we’re looking at the reported mix of signups. We’ve moved from the size of the audience, to how often people sent invitations, to where new accounts came from.

Suppose you’re looking at those snapshots and wondering where the first 100,000 users came from. The figures on that slide leave that question open. The early milestone is dated September 2008. The signup mix is what Houston presented in his later talk. Applying that percentage backward would assume Dropbox’s mix of acquisition channels stayed the same through a period when the company was changing how it grew.

*Visual: Fixed milestones on a timeline, invitation volume on its own card, then a separate breakdown of reported daily signups. Give each chart its own denominator.*

## Back at the desk

Look one last time at what Alex and Sam are doing. When Sam opens the folder, the act of using the product is already a chance to discover it. When Sam receives the referral, Dropbox adds another reason to try it. Houston later recalled working on friction throughout the sequence from using Dropbox, to sharing or referring, to signing up. The invitation was one part of that sequence.

The choice worth remembering is the reward itself. Dropbox gave people more of what they came to use. For Alex, that meant more room for files. For Sam, the invitation came with something useful on the first day. One button could hand both people a reason to care.

*Visual: Sam opens the shared file. Return to the invitation receipt beside it, with a simple label for each path.*

## What the team could learn

Imagine that you can see only one line on a dashboard: total registered users. The line goes up after the invitation feature ships. You might think the invitation explains the rise. But the shared folder is still out there, and people can still arrive through other routes. The total will show the combined change. It cannot tell you which action to make easier next.

Now follow the two paths independently. On the folder path, the first question is whether Sam can get to the file Alex meant to share. On the invitation path, the first question is whether Sam understands why joining is worth the trouble. In both cases, you can watch people move from one step to the next and see where they stop. That’s how a founder can turn a broad idea about word of mouth into choices about a specific button, message, or signup screen.

Here’s the decision this case leaves with a builder. Start with what one person already wants to send. Give the person receiving it a reason to care, and measure that path from the first click through signup. When you introduce a reward, track the action it changes. Dropbox’s story is strongest where you can see those small product choices alongside the numbers the team reported.

*Visual: Reprise the desktop. Follow each path on its own, then show the two reasons to click side by side.*

## What the numbers cannot prove

None of these figures is a controlled experiment. The deck reports registration snapshots, a thirty-day invitation count, and the share of signups the company assigned to different channels. It does not give us the probability that any single invite converted, or tell us which channel caused each early milestone. A business can grow while several distribution features are changing at the same time. That makes a story tempting, but not every neat explanation is a measured one. The useful lesson is more concrete: show which user action introduces the product, which action asks someone new to join, and exactly what each number counts. If those are separate questions in the actual product, they deserve separate measures in the story too.

*Visual: Three distinctly labelled evidence panels. Users registered, invitations sent and signups attributed to a channel must never share an implied denominator.*

## Reading the result

A founder looking at these numbers today would want to keep the time windows in view. The invitation count covers thirty days. The two registration figures are snapshots many months apart. Put all three on the same chart and their different meanings disappear. Give each its own label and the viewer can see both the scale of the company and the activity behind one product feature.

The other thing to keep in view is what the reward asks people to do. In our illustration, Alex already has a file to send and Sam already has a reason to open it. A referral is a separate request. That distinction gives a builder a testable question: does the reward help someone take a step they were otherwise unlikely to take?

## Closing

A shared file gives someone a task to finish. A referral gives both people an incentive to start. That’s the difference hidden inside two clicks on the same desktop.
